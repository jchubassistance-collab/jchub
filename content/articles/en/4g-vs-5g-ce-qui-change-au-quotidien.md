Ads promise “instant” downloads and a revolution. In real life, many people who switched to 5G barely notice a difference. Both are true: 5G brings real progress, but not always where you’d expect. Here’s what concretely changes.

## What 5G really improves

**1. Capacity.** It’s its biggest strength, and the least visible. 5G handles many more devices connected at the same time in the same area. At a stadium, a market, a concert, where 4G saturates and “nothing goes through”, 5G copes better with the load.

**2. Speed.** 5G’s theoretical peak speeds are counted in gigabits per second. In practice, you often get a few hundred Mbit/s in good conditions, against a few dozen on 4G. Useful to download a big file or a series, much less so to read a message.

**3. Latency.** 5G can react faster than 4G, which benefits online games, video calls and some professional uses. But that very low latency depends on how the operator built its network: many 5G networks still rely on the 4G core network, and the gain then stays modest.

## What barely changes

- **WhatsApp, social media, browsing**: good 4G is already more than enough. A slow web page is often slow because of the site itself, not the network.
- **Video streaming**: HD needs only a few Mbit/s; 4G delivers them easily.
- **Indoor coverage**: depending on the frequencies used, 5G can struggle through walls, and your phone then falls back to 4G.

## Why not all “5G” is equal

5G uses several frequency bands, each with different trade-offs:

- **low frequencies** travel far and go through walls well, but with a limited speed gain;
- **mid frequencies**, around 3.5 GHz, offer the best balance: it’s the 5G “you can feel”;
- **millimeter waves**, very fast but very short-range, remain rare and limited to specific places.

So the little “5G” logo on your screen doesn’t tell you which 5G you’re getting.

## What you need to benefit from it

Three conditions, all required:

1. **A 5G phone**, compatible with the frequencies your operator uses.
2. **A plan** that includes 5G.
3. **5G coverage** where you are. Rollouts usually start with city centers; check your operator’s coverage map.

If one is missing, your phone uses 4G, which remains very good for most uses.

## Battery, the question everyone asks

The first 5G phones drained noticeably more. Recent models have improved a lot, but a phone constantly searching for a weak 5G signal, at the edge of coverage, runs down faster. If your battery melts in a poorly covered area, forcing 4G mode in the network settings can help.

## 5G at home

One use is growing: **home Internet over 5G**. A box receives the 5G signal and spreads Wi-Fi through the house, with no cable or fiber to the home. Where fiber hasn’t arrived yet, it’s often an interesting alternative. Place the box near a window, on the side facing the antenna.

## So, should you switch to 5G?

- **You’re changing phones anyway**: get a 5G model, it will stay current longer.
- **Your 4G phone works well**: no rush. 4G will stay available for many years.
- **You work with big files or on the move** in a covered area: 5G will save you real time.
- **You need a home connection** without fiber available: look at 5G box offers.

## Key takeaways

- 5G’s real progress is **capacity**: it handles crowds better.
- Speed and latency improve, but for everyday use, good 4G is enough.
- You need a compatible phone, a 5G plan **and** coverage.
- Not all “5G” is equal: the frequency used changes everything.
